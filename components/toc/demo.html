Why we moved off the monolith

For six years the shop ran as one Rails application. It served us well: one deploy, one database, one place to look when something broke. By the spring, though, a release took most of a day, and every team waited on every other team.

We did not want a rewrite. We wanted to take one piece out, run it on its own, and learn what that costs before taking out the next.

Picking the first service

The first candidate had to be small, well understood and boring. Exciting candidates teach you about the domain; boring ones teach you about the move itself, which was the point.

Looking for a seam

We listed every module with its callers and its tables. Invoicing stood out: three callers, two tables, and a clear verb at its edge ("make an invoice for this order").

Search, which everyone wanted to move first, had forty callers. It stayed.

Keeping the database, for now

Splitting the data on day one would have doubled the risk. The new service read and wrote the same invoice tables through a narrow repository, and nothing else was allowed to touch them.

What "done" meant

Done meant: the monolith no longer contains invoice code, the service deploys on its own in under ten minutes, and an on-call engineer who never saw it can find the logs.

Running both side by side

For three weeks every invoice was made twice: once by the monolith, once by the service, which only logged its result. A nightly job compared the two.

The differences we found

Rounding, of course. The old code rounded each line, the new code the total. Neither was wrong, but the customer-facing number had to stay the same, so the service learned the old habit.

Time zones came second: invoices made just before midnight landed on different days.

Switching over

We switched one country at a time behind a flag, starting with the smallest. The flag stayed for a month after the last country, then went in a one-line commit that got more applause than any feature that year.

What it cost

Four months for two people, where we had planned six weeks. Most of the extra time went into the plumbing every later service will reuse: the deploy pipeline, tracing across the boundary, and a local setup that starts both apps with one command.

What we would do again

Pick the boring candidate. Run old and new side by side long enough to be bored by the comparison report. Move one country, or one customer, at a time.

And write down what "done" means before starting: it is the only thing that stopped the project from quietly becoming a rewrite.

Levels and deep entries

levels takes a range (2-3) or a list (2,4); headings takes any CSS selector instead. nested="active" shows the deeper entries only under the section being read.

Folded

collapse="always" folds the list behind a toggle that shows the current section; collapse="auto" does so below collapse-below (default 48rem), as the sidebar above does on a phone. Picking an entry or Escape closes it.

Hand-written entries

Without headings to read, the entries come from list-data ({ "title", "href" | "id", "children" }, never "data") or from a nested list of links inside the element, which is also what shows before the script loads. Links to # ids on the page take part in the scrollspy; these point into the article above.
